Excessive claims

  • Don't make statements about performance or cost that aren't easily verifiable with data that's available to the reader.
    • Bad: Our product is faster than ExampleCorp's product.
    • Good: Our product distributes datasets and computation in memory across a cluster, and therefore it can be faster for this scenario than ExampleCorporation's product. For more information, see Performance comparison.
  • If you make specific performance claims—how fast a product is, how much storage it requires, and so on—reference the source of your information.
    • Bad: Our product is faster than ExampleCorp's product.
    • Good: Our product distributes datasets and computation in memory across a cluster, and therefore it can be faster for this scenario than ExampleCorporation's product. For more information, see Performance comparison.
  • Don't make a statement about security that would be invalidated by a security incident; suggest that a feature "helps with security" or "is designed for security" instead of claiming that a product is secure.
    • Bad: Our security product prevents account takeovers from phishing attacks.
    • Good: Using our security product is part of an overall strategy that helps prevent account takeovers from phishing attacks.
  • Don't make statements that might be interpreted as subjective or even disparaging, especially about third-party products.
  • When you describe products, avoid superlatives like best, simplest, fastest, never, and always.
  • Be careful about words like ensure and guarantee, and use them only when something can truly be ensured or guaranteed.
  • Write factually and objectively, limiting what you say to verifiable information that will be true over the lifespan of your documentation, not just what's true today.
