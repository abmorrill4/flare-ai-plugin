Inclusive language

Avoid unnecessarily gendered language

  • Be mindful of the pronouns that are used in narrative examples, and be aware of other possible sources of gendered language.
    • Bad: Equipment installation takes around 16 man-hours to complete.
    • Good: Equipment installation takes around 16 person-hours to complete.

Avoid figurative language

  • Use simple language and terminology that's precise and clear for all of your audiences.
    • Bad: You can allowlist a range of IP addresses by entering a CIDR block instead of a single address in the field.
    • Good: You can allow requests from a range of IP addresses by entering a CIDR block instead of a single address in the field.
  • Avoid idiomatic or figurative language that can be misunderstood, distracting, or difficult for translation.
  • Avoid jargon.
  • Use terms that are established industry standards and widely understood by the target audience.
  • Be attentive to your word choice, especially when you aim for an informal tone, so that you don't use figures of speech and other turns of phrase.
  • Don't use metaphors, and don't use a term in a metaphorical sense; use words in their primary sense (for example, avoid the metaphor of pets versus cattle).

Avoid ableist language

  • Avoid ableist language such as crazy, insane, blind to or blind eye to, cripple, and dumb; choose a more accurate or alternative word, depending on the context.
    • Bad: There are some crazy outliers in the data.
    • Good: There are some baffling outliers in the data.
AvoidUseSeverityWhyExample
dumb, blind eye, sanity-check, dummywarningAbleist language; choose a more accurate word.Before launch, give everything a final sanity-check.

Avoid graphic or metaphorical language

  • Avoid unnecessarily graphic or metaphorical language, when you can use a more precise term.
    • Bad: If the connection hangs, check for errors.
    • Good: If the connection doesn't respond, check for errors.
  • Instead of STONITH, use specific terms to describe the process that's used to stop an errant node.
    • Good: This approach might require you to fence failed nodes.
  • If you need to mention a term such as STONITH, mention it once when you first explain the relevant feature, and phrase it in a way that de-emphasizes the term.
    • Good: This approach might require you to fence failed nodes (sometimes referred to as STONITH).
  • Always use the most precise and well-understood terms for your context, keeping an industry-established term when it has a specific technical meaning without an accurate synonym.

Use precise, objective language

  • Use precise, objective language so that all readers can gather the same information from your writing; don't use vague or subjective language.
  • Avoid words like simple, simply, easy, easily, quick, and quickly, which introduce an opinion that might not reflect the user's experience.
    • Bad: Simply open the configuration file to quickly add your custom key.
    • Good: Open the configuration file and add your custom key.
  • Avoid words like careful, carefully, thorough, and thoroughly, which provide unclear instruction or imply that the user is usually careless or inattentive.
    • Bad: Make sure that you carefully and thoroughly review all of the audit logs for potential security issues.
    • Good: Review the audit logs for security issues. For example, ensure that the user accounts have multi-factor authentication enabled.
  • Avoid words like proper and properly, which are unclear and can assert opinions of superiority.
    • Bad: Ensure the daemon is properly installed and running.
    • Good: To verify that the daemon is running, run systemctl status DAEMON_NAME and confirm that the output shows Active: active (running).
AvoidUseSeverityWhyExample
simply, easily, quickly, carefully, thoroughly, properlywarningSubjective or unclear wording.Simply open the configuration file to quickly add your custom key.

Write diverse and inclusive examples

  • Write documentation for a global audience, and use diverse names, genders, ages, and locations in examples.
  • Follow the gender-neutral pronoun guidance in examples.
  • Avoid being too culturally specific to the US, and be mindful when referring to specific holidays, cultural practices, sports, and figures of speech.
  • In examples, choose a diverse set of names to help reflect our global audience.
  • When writing about older adults, avoid terms and figures of speech such as the elderly, the aged, seniors, senior citizens, or 80 years young; use terms such as older adults or aging population, or mention relative age or relationship when relevant.

Write about features and users in inclusive ways

  • Avoid referring to people in divisive ways, such as native speakers or non-native speakers of English; discuss the feature in terms that are relevant to anyone.
  • Avoid using socially charged terms for technical concepts where possible, such as blacklist, native feature, and first-class citizen.
AvoidUseSeverityWhyExample
native featurewarningSocially charged term for a technical concept.For example, avoid terms such as blacklist and native feature, and don't use terms like first-class citizen, even though such terms might still be widely used.

Replace established terms

  • If replacing an established non-inclusive term could cause confusion, refer to the non-inclusive term on the first use only, and put it in parentheses.
    • Good: To make sure that administrators get the notification, add them to an allowlist (sometimes called a whitelist). Anyone who isn't on the allowlist is blocked ...
  • After the first use, use the inclusive, replacement term throughout the rest of the document.
    • Good: In this model, a Jenkins controller (master) handles HTTP requests. The Jenkins controller is designed to ...

Write around non-inclusive code terms

  • Don't use a non-inclusive name or keyword unless it's in code font.
  • Minimize your use of a non-inclusive term embedded in code, while still providing clear documentation to your readers.
  • The first time that you refer to a code item that uses a non-inclusive term, format the term in code font, and put it in parentheses if possible.
    • Good: The configuration file helps you create a parent node (which is named master in the file).
  • In subsequent mentions, use the preferred term, and if it's necessary to refer to the entity name or keyword, do so only with code formatting.
    • Good: Start the replica by using the START SLAVE statement.

Avoid bias and harm when discussing disability and accessibility

  • When writing about people with disabilities or about accessibility, work to eliminate unintentional bias and harm.
  • Don't describe people without disabilities as normal or healthy; use terms such as nondisabled person, sighted person, hearing person, person without disabilities, or neurotypical person.
  • Use the terms that the communities you're writing about prefer, and in many cases avoid terms that remove personhood or define people by their disability, such as the disabled or a quadriplegic.
  • Use see to refer to links and cross-references.
  • Avoid terms that reflect or project feelings and judgments about a person's disability, such as victim of, suffering from, or wheelchair-bound; use neutral terms such as experiencing, living with, or uses a wheelchair.
  • Avoid euphemisms or patronizing terms such as physically challenged, special, differently abled, or handi-capable.
